\title{When Tooth-Numbering Detectors Fail Together: A Position-Only Baseline and Closed Gaps on Panoramic X-rays}
\titlerunning{When Tooth-Numbering Detectors Fail Together}

\begin{abstract}
Tooth-numbering detectors for panoramic X-rays reach high accuracy, but the format puts each tooth in a predictable place, so some of that accuracy could come from position alone. We measure this with a
position-only baseline: a classifier that sees each tooth's bounding box and
no pixels. On 425 uncropped X-rays (11,602 teeth), with cross-validation grouped by X-ray, it numbers 78.5\% of teeth correctly.
Three detectors (YOLOv8x, RT-DETR-l, Faster R-CNN) beat
it by 14.3 to 16.7 percentage points, on all 32 tooth numbers, and this
holds on two more splits. Their rare shared mistakes still follow position.
On 1.4\% of teeth the three detectors and the baseline are all wrong and the
detectors agree on one wrong number; in 91\% of these the baseline gives the
same number, against under 7\% by chance. These teeth are five times as
likely as correct teeth to sit next to a missing tooth, and the wrong number
is usually the missing tooth's number. They occur where the neighbors have closed the space. Closing a gap on purpose reproduces them: when a neighbor is moved into an erased tooth's space, all three detectors give it the missing number 21\% of the time, against 0.2\% when it is left open. On 634 DENTEX X-rays, detectors trained there beat position alone by 22 to 25 points, their shared errors again give the baseline's answer (85.5\%), and closing a gap reproduces them 12 to 16\% of the time. We release the per-tooth table and a scorer.
\keywords{Tooth numbering \and Panoramic radiography \and Shortcut learning \and Error consistency}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Assigning each tooth its FDI number is the first step of most automated
dental charting: a caries or a periapical lesion found by a detector is
only useful once it is attached to the right tooth. Public benchmarks such
as DENTEX~\cite{hamamci2023dentex} score numbering together with detection, and detectors reach high numbering accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

Panoramic X-rays are a standardized format. The patient bites on a guide,
the arch runs left to right across the image, and each tooth number has a
typical location. This invites a shortcut~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}: a
model can get many numbers right from where a box sits, without reading the
tooth. Such a shortcut would not show in overall accuracy, and it would fail
on patients who differ from the typical arch, for example after extractions.

We ask how much of tooth numbering position alone explains, and whether
detectors fail where position fails. Our contributions are:
(1)~a position-only baseline, trained in seconds on six box features, that
numbers 78.5\% of teeth correctly under grouped cross-validation, with three
detectors beating it by 14 to 17 points on every tooth number;
(2)~an error analysis showing that the errors the detectors share are
position-like;
(3)~experiments (image shift, context masking, tooth erasure, and moving a
neighbor into the space of an erased tooth) that place these errors at
closed gaps, next to a missing tooth whose neighbors have moved together so
that the arch looks complete; and
(4)~a released per-tooth benchmark with folds, reference predictions and a
scorer.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Tooth numbering.} Detection-based numbering on panoramic X-rays is
standard, with DENTEX~\cite{hamamci2023dentex} and diffusion-based
detectors~\cite{hamamci2023hierarchical} as recent examples. DENTEX challenge entries combine segmentation with detection~\cite{he2023dentex}, build a YOLO-based framework~\cite{mei2023yolortho}, or use Mask R-CNN with a DiffusionDet and DINO ensemble~\cite{choi2023detdet}. Some methods
use the arch layout as an explicit prior~\cite{budagam2024oralbbnet}.
Evaluations report accuracy or mAP; we found none that compares a model with
what position alone achieves on the same teeth.

\textbf{Shortcut learning.} Models can rely on cues that correlate with the
label in the training data, from skin markings in
dermoscopy~\cite{winkler2019markings} to site-specific features in chest
X-rays~\cite{degrave2021covid} and segmentation~\cite{lin2024shortcut}; see
also~\cite{hill2024shortcutting,cajas2026shortkit}. These studies show a
shortcut by removing or altering the cue. We use the same logic for a cue
that is partly legitimate: position does carry information about tooth
number.

\textbf{Error consistency.} Whether two models fail on the same inputs
beyond chance is measured by error-consistency
kappa~\cite{geirhos2020consistency}. We go one step further and compare the
wrong answers themselves.

\section{Methods}

\textbf{Data and splits.} UFBA-425~\cite{budagam2025ufba} has 425 panoramic
X-rays ($512\times512$ pixels) with a mask for each tooth, labeled by FDI
number (32 classes, third molars included). A tooth's box is the full extent
of its mask, which gives 11,602 teeth. We use the original X-rays only. An
augmented export of this data adds randomly cropped copies, which move teeth
within the frame and lowered position-only accuracy by about 8 points in our
earlier splits. The X-rays are split into five folds balanced by the
dataset's categories. Each training fold holds out 15\% of its X-rays for
model selection, and every X-ray is tested once, by models that never saw
it. Two further splits (other random seeds) share at most 26\% and 32\% of any test fold with the first.

\textbf{Data use.} Both datasets are public and were used as released; we
collected no patient data. UFBA-425 is a set of anonymized X-rays on Figshare
under CC BY 4.0; we found no ethics statement for it. DENTEX is released by
its authors under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, and they report ethics committee approval
for the X-rays from three hospitals~\cite{hamamci2023dentex}.

\textbf{Models.} The position-only baseline is scikit-learn's gradient-boosted
tree classifier (Hist\-Gradient\-Boosting, default settings) on six features of
the labeled box: center $x$ and $y$, width, height, area and aspect ratio,
all relative to image size. It never sees pixels. The detectors are
YOLOv8x~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}, RT-DETR-l~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr} and Faster
R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster} with a ResNet-50 FPN
backbone~\cite{li2021benchmarking}, all starting from COCO weights. They are
a single-stage CNN, a transformer and a two-stage CNN, from two separate code
bases. Each is trained for 30 epochs with horizontal flips off (a flip would
swap left and right tooth numbers). YOLOv8x and RT-DETR-l read 640-pixel
input; Faster R-CNN uses torchvision's default resizing (short side 800
pixels) and SGD with a learning rate scaled to its batch of 2. We keep the
epoch with the best validation fitness
($0.1\,\mathrm{mAP}_{50} + 0.9\,\mathrm{mAP}_{50:95}$).

\textbf{Scoring.} Detections with confidence of at least 0.5 are matched
one-to-one to labeled teeth at IoU of at least 0.5, highest confidence
first, whatever number they give. A tooth with no match counts as wrong.
Top-1 is the share of labeled teeth that are matched and given the right
number; this puts the detectors and the baseline, which always answers, on
the same teeth. We also report numbered F1, which also penalizes extra
boxes. AP50 and AP50:95 are in the code release; top-1 stays the headline
because the baseline has no scores. CIs are 95\% intervals from 10,000
bootstrap draws of X-rays, paired when two models are compared. The decision
rule for each experiment was written and frozen before it was run.

A tooth is a \emph{joint failure} when the position-only model and all three
detectors are wrong, no detector missed the tooth, and the three detectors
give the same number. The \emph{position match} is the share of joint
failures where the position-only model gives that same number. Its chance
level comes from shuffling the position-only answers among the joint
failures.

\textbf{Interventions.} \emph{Shift:} the whole image is moved sideways by
10\% of its width (about two tooth widths), and the labels move with it.
\emph{Context masking:} everything outside a tooth's box, widened by $k$ box
widths and heights ($k=0.5,1,2$), is blacked out. \emph{Missing neighbor:}
the next position in the arch has no labeled tooth. \emph{Tooth erasure:}
for up to three teeth per X-ray with both neighbors present, the tooth's
mask (grown by 3 px) is filled in by inpainting~\cite{telea2004inpainting},
and we ask whether a neighbor that was numbered right now takes the erased
tooth's number. Controls are the teeth two places away and a sham inpaint
over bone. \emph{Space closure:} for each missing tooth with both neighbors
present, $S$ is the shortest distance between the neighbors' masks divided
by the missing tooth's median width ($S=0$ when they touch). \emph{Gap
closure:} after erasing a tooth, its distal neighbor (the next one toward
the back of the mouth) is cut out and pasted back shifted toward the gap, by
half or all of the distance at which it touches the tooth on the other side,
with its box moved to match. In a variant, \emph{tipping}, the neighbor is
instead rotated about its root apex until its crown touches.

\textbf{Position explains most; detectors add 14 to 17 points.}
Position alone numbers more than three in four teeth (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
Each detector beats it on all 32 tooth numbers. The gap is smallest at the
third molars (4.3 to 5.8 points) and largest at the lower central incisors
(up to 25.5). If the baseline is given the detectors' own boxes instead of
the labeled ones, it does slightly better (79.0 to 80.2\%) and the gap is
14.5 to 16.2 points, so the labeled boxes do not inflate its score. On the
two further splits, every gap falls inside the first split's CI. YOLOv8x and
RT-DETR-l tie on top-1 (difference 0.17 points, where the smallest
difference we could detect is 0.50), but RT-DETR-l leaves about 1.9
unmatched boxes per X-ray against 0.4 for YOLOv8x, which lowers its F1 by
2.7 points.

\textbf{Shared errors follow position.} 161 teeth (1.4\%) are joint
failures. In 91.3\% of them the position-only model gives the same wrong
number as the detectors; shuffling its answers gives at most 6.8\% (95th
percentile). In 94.4\% the wrong number is the next tooth over in the same
quadrant. The detectors are about as confident on these teeth as on correct
ones (AUROC 0.53 to 0.66, where 0.5 is chance), so a confidence cutoff does
not find them, and neither does a rule based on the detectors' second
choices. About 10\% of joint failures may be label
errors~\cite{northcutt2021confident}; without them the position match is
91.7\%.

\textbf{Not position in the frame, not distant teeth.} Shifting the image by
two tooth widths leaves the position-only model right on under 9\% of teeth,
but changes the detectors' top-1 by under 0.3 points. Blacking out everything
beyond one box width costs Faster R-CNN and RT-DETR-l about 26 points
(YOLOv8x 9.6), counting teeth that are no longer detected as wrong. Even so,
among the joint failures that are still detected, 74 to 93\% keep the shared
wrong number.

\textbf{Next to missing teeth.} 44.7\% of joint failures have a missing
neighbor, against 8.5\% of teeth that all three detectors get right (ratio
5.3, CI 4.2 to 6.6). In 67 of the 72 joint failures next to a gap (93\%), the
wrong number is the missing tooth's. Teeth that only the position-only model
gets wrong have a missing neighbor 10.5\% of the time, so gaps are not where
position fails in general, only where position and the detectors fail
together.

\textbf{At closed gaps.} Across 372 missing teeth with both neighbors
present, the space left between the neighbors separates gaps with a joint
failure from gaps without one (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}; AUROC 0.78, CI 0.70 to
0.84; median $S$ 0.00 against 0.32). 56\% of these errors sit at gaps whose
neighbors touch, against 20\% of other gaps. Each detector's own errors
follow closure about as well (AUROC 0.74 to 0.79), and better than the
position-only model's (0.68).

\textbf{An open gap is not enough.} After a tooth is erased, a correctly
numbered neighbor takes its number in 2.0\% (YOLOv8x), 4.4\% (RT-DETR-l) and
5.7\% (Faster R-CNN) of cases, against 0 to 0.2\% for both controls. But all
three detectors do it to the same neighbor in only 4 of 2,260 cases (0.18\%).

\textbf{Closing the gap reproduces the shared error.} For the same erased
teeth, we moved the distal neighbor sideways until it touched the tooth on
the other side. Of 1,114 moved teeth that all three detectors numbered right
on the intact X-ray, all three gave the missing tooth's number for 0.18\%
with the space open, 2.9\% when moved halfway and 20.8\% when moved all the
way (20.6 points above open, CI 18.2 to 23.1). Each detector alone did so
38 to 42\% of the time. Models trained on the two further splits give the
same result (22.5\% and 21.8\% at full closure, at most 0.1\% open). Tipping
the neighbor, which is closer to how real teeth drift, gives 9.4\% (CI 7.7 to 11.0; 9.3\% on the second split's models). At real gaps whose neighbors almost touch ($S\le0.1$), 22\% hold a joint failure, about 11\% per neighbor. Cutting the tooth out and pasting it back
unmoved gives 0.0\%, so the pasted edges do not cause the effect. In these cases the moved tooth looks the same as before, yet the detectors number it by the slot it now occupies.

\textbf{A second dataset.} We repeated the main steps on 634 DENTEX X-rays
(18,095 teeth) from other clinics~\cite{hamamci2023dentex}. Position alone
numbers 66.9\% of these teeth. The UFBA-trained detectors, run unchanged,
lose accuracy under this shift (YOLOv8x misses many teeth; Faster R-CNN is
often one position off), though their shared errors still follow position
(87.5\%). We therefore also trained all three on DENTEX with five-fold
cross-validation. They then beat position alone by 22 to 25 points. Their 303
shared errors give the baseline's answer 85.5\% of the time (UFBA: 91.3\%)
and sit next to a missing tooth 3.5 times as often as correct teeth (UFBA:
5.3). Both fall short of the targets we set in advance (85.7\% and 4.2), so we call this a partial replication. Two more training runs on the
same folds give the same picture: margins of 24 to 26 points, a position
match of 85.5\% and 84.2\%, and a gap ratio of 3.3 and 3.2; 213 of the 410
teeth that are a shared error in any run are one in all three. Part of the weaker gap link depends on which X-rays are in the data: after weighting DENTEX to UFBA's mix of missing teeth per X-ray, the ratio is 3.9. The interventions also work on DENTEX, with smaller effects (tables in the code release). At each X-ray's stored size,
sliding a neighbor into the erased tooth's space gives the missing number
15.7\% of the time (0.4\% open) and tipping it 7.0\% (0.4\% open), about
three quarters of the UFBA rates (one training run). On X-rays shrunk to 640 pixels wide the rates are lower (slide 11.8\%, 12.0\% and 12.6\%, tip 4.8\%, 5.5\% and 6.5\% on three training runs; our target of 5\% for the tip was met on two of the three), mostly because Faster R-CNN reads the shrunken X-rays less well. The individual teeth that fill differ between runs: of 341 slide fills in any of the three runs, 57 occur in all three.

\section{Discussion}

The detectors read teeth: shifting the image barely changes their answers, and they beat the position-only model on every tooth number. Yet their rare
shared errors are the position-only model's errors, and our results suggest
where they come from. When a tooth is missing and its neighbors have moved
together, the arch shows no gap. Counting along it gives the tooth next to
the closed space the missing tooth's number, and that tooth also sits roughly
where the missing one would. Appearance, context and position then point the
same way, and every model follows. An erased tooth leaves a full-width empty
space, which does not create this agreement; closing the space does.

Two practical points follow. First, a position-only baseline is cheap and
should be reported with tooth-numbering results: on standardized panoramic
X-rays it sets a high floor, and the margin above it shows what a model adds.
Second, confidence does not find the errors that models share. A check from
outside the model works better: look at the numbering next to every missing
tooth, especially where the space has closed. In our data this check covers
97\% of the shared errors at gaps, but it also flags many gaps without one
(precision 13\%), because most gaps are partly closed. A version that uses
only a model's own output flags teeth next to a position the model left
empty, and teeth that share a number. It flags 12 to 16\% of teeth and
catches 41 to 47\% of the shared errors on every split, against 17 to 32\%
for the same number of least-confident teeth. We had set a target of at
least 1.5 times as many as confidence, which it reached on two of three
splits. Training with simulated missing and closed teeth did not remove the
error: YOLOv8x numbered teeth next to real gaps no better than a retrained
control ($+0.1$ points, CI $-1.6$ to $1.7$).

\textbf{Limitations.} UFBA is one dataset from one source, with a single
annotation per tooth and no patient data. The DENTEX replication is partial,
and its stored-size intervention results rest on one training run. The
interventions move or tip a tooth within the image plane only, but real
teeth also rotate, and outlines that touch in a flat image may not touch in
the mouth. Third molars and longer gaps, which hold most missing positions,
are outside the closure analysis. The rates are stable across splits and
training runs, but the individual teeth are not: about a third of the joint
failures change when the same model is retrained (overlap 0.63, against 0.54
to 0.57 across splits). About 10\% of joint failures may be label errors.

\section{Conclusion}

On uncropped panoramic X-rays, position alone numbers 78.5\% of teeth and
detectors add 14 to 17 points. The few errors they share follow position and
concentrate next to missing teeth whose neighbors have closed the space. On a
second dataset, DENTEX, the same pattern holds with a weaker link to gaps and
smaller intervention effects. We release the per-tooth table and scorer so
that new models can be measured against the baseline and on these shared
failures.
